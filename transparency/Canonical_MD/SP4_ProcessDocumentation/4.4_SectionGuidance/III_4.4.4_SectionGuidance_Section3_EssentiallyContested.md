# Section Guidance: Section 3 - Why Engage with AI-Assisted Scholarship?

## COMPLETE REWRITE - Stage III Revision

**Target length:** 800-1000 words

**Core task:** Replace the current Section 3 entirely. The new section focuses on ONE reason: *we need to track what philosophy is becoming* because the question of whether AI changes philosophy is unanswerable without settling what philosophy is—and philosophy is an essentially contested concept.

---

## The Argumentative Structure

### The Setup (2-3 paragraphs)

**Open with the empirical observation:**
The practice of philosophy is changing. Scholars increasingly work with AI systems in ways that were impossible a decade ago. This is not speculation—it is happening.

**Pose the natural question:**
Does this change philosophy *continuously* (like word processors, search engines, or digital archives) or *discontinuously* (fundamentally altering what the activity is)?

**The impasse:**
This question cannot be answered directly. Whether AI assistance changes philosophy depends on what features of philosophy are *constitutive* versus merely *regulative*—what defines the activity versus what merely governs how it's done.

### The Analogy: Nature-of-Activities (2-3 paragraphs)

**Draw on Santoni de Sio et al. (2016) on enhancement:**

The debate about cognitive enhancement faces a parallel problem. Does taking modafinil while studying change what "studying" is? The answer depends on:

1. **Constitutive vs regulative rules:** Some rules define an activity (constitutive); others merely regulate how it's done. Running a marathon on roller-skates isn't running a marathon—the prohibition on wheels is constitutive. But the 90-minute length of football games is arbitrary—changing it wouldn't change the nature of the game.

2. **Coarse-grained vs fine-grained descriptions:** On a coarse-grained description, 1950s car racing and today's car racing are "the same sport." On a fine-grained description—considering technology, driving techniques, embedded systems—they are significantly different activities.

**The contested middle ground:**
Most rules "fall somewhere between these two extremes. They are neither as vital as the ban on wheels in marathons nor as arbitrary as allocating 90 minutes to football games" (Santoni de Sio et al., 2016, p. 34).

**Apply to philosophy:**
Is unaided cognition constitutive of philosophy, or merely regulative? Is writing one's own sentences constitutive, or could philosophy be done through prompt engineering and iterative refinement? These are not empirical questions with neutral answers—they are *contested*.

### The Meta-Level Move: Essentially Contested Concepts (2 paragraphs)

**Invoke Gallie (1956):**
"Philosophy" is what Gallie called an *essentially contested concept*—a concept whose proper use is itself a matter of ongoing dispute among competent users. What counts as "doing philosophy" is not fixed by definition but is itself a philosophical question.

**The consequence:**
We cannot settle whether AI fundamentally changes philosophy without first settling what philosophy is. But settling what philosophy is would require resolving disputes that are constitutive of the discipline itself. The question "Does AI change philosophy?" presupposes an answer to a question that has no neutral adjudication.

### The Reframing: From Answer to Tracking (3-4 paragraphs)

**The pivot:**
If we cannot answer whether AI changes philosophy, what *can* we do?

We can **track what philosophy is becoming**. We can create conditions under which the community can observe, compare, and evaluate different modes of philosophical production. We can enable informed *meta-philosophical* discussion rather than presupposing a particular answer.

**The compounding uncertainty - unknown future skills:**
The uncertainty runs deeper still. Even setting aside the essentially contested nature of philosophy, we do not know what AI-assisted philosophical *practice* will look like. Initially, the focus is on *prompting*—formulating queries that elicit useful outputs. But practice is already evolving toward *steering*—iteratively guiding AI through complex argumentative terrain. Perhaps philosophers will become *architecture builders*—designing intellectual structures that AI systems populate with content. We simply do not know.

This compounds the uncertainty. We face not only the question "what is philosophy?" but also "what will the relevant philosophical skills be under AI assistance?" If we cannot anticipate whether the salient human contribution will be prompting, steering, architectural design, or something not yet imagined, we certainly cannot prejudge whether that contribution preserves or transforms the nature of philosophical work.

**What this requires:**
Tracking requires *visibility*. If AI-assisted work is produced opaquely—if the process by which arguments are developed, refined, and selected remains hidden—then the community has no basis for assessing what is happening to philosophical practice. Transparency is not a bureaucratic requirement but the precondition for the very discussion we need to have.

**The nesting:**
This frames everything else the paper proposes:
- **Meaningful human control** (Section 6) operationalizes what it means for philosophical work to remain attributable to human intellectual agency
- **Epistemic integrity** (verification, replicability) ensures that claims produced under AI assistance can be trusted
- Both are *instrumental* to the higher goal: enabling the community to track, assess, and deliberate about what philosophy is becoming

### The Conclusion (1 paragraph)

**Close with the stakes:**
The question is not whether we can stop philosophy from changing—we cannot. The question is whether the change will be *visible* enough for the community to understand it, *documented* enough for meaningful comparison, and *attributable* enough for intellectual responsibility to be maintained. The transparency requirements proposed in this paper are designed to make that possible.
