## Finding 1: Origin of the costly signaling argument

**Location:** Feature vs. Bug debate, user's intervention near the end of the conversation.

After AI ranked the Feature (barrier is good) and Bug (barrier is bad) arguments against each other, the user intervened:

> "I disagree integrity is fully protected by commitment and damaged by wrong incentives. The stronger arguments to me appear those based on structural constraints that use human effort as costly signal."

This is the first articulation of the costly signaling criterion that appears in Section 5 of the CFP paper (ecological validity, good faith, costly signaling). It did not originate in the JPEP writing sessions. It originated here, as the user's analytical reframe of the publishing barrier debate.

AI reranked accordingly, with costly signal / preventing flood as the strongest Feature argument — and then the user's engagement revealed the signal's structural weaknesses (unverifiable, arbitrary threshold, already violated by accepted low-cost assistance).

**SP-3 implication:** Section 5's costly signaling criterion has a traceable prior. SP-3 should locate its origin here, not in the JPEP development sessions. The argument was not derived within the JPEP framework — it was brought into it from prior thinking.

## Finding 2: Origin of the transparency paradox / laundering concept

**Location:** Discussion of publication compliance for AI-assisted arguments.

The conversation explicitly named and analysed what it called the "laundering requirement":

> "Arguments substantially developed through AI collaboration must be 'cleaned' through human rewriting to enter formal venues. This means: the collaborative process is erased — it appears as purely human work."

The user's framing: current rules create a structural barrier to entry for ideas "too synthetic" in origin — they can circulate informally but cannot enter formal discourse without human reformulation that obscures their genesis.

This is the motivating problem of the JPEP paper — mandatory disclosure is structurally penalised, the process is systematically opaque. Its first articulation predates the founding conversation (da6a830c) and the entire JPEP documentation framework.

The user also made clear their personal position: "I left academia. I want to explore whether current rules prevent some ideas from being discussed." This is the author's epistemic stance entering the project: an outsider examining a structural problem, not a participant seeking compliance.

**SP-3 implication:** The transparency paradox was not invented in the JPEP writing sessions. It was observed and named here, in a conversation about a different paper (the Mackie error theory), as a structural fact about AI-assisted academic work. SP-3 should acknowledge this.
