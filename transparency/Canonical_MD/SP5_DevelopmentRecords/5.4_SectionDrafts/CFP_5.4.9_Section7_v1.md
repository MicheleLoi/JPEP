# 7. Community Assessment of Documentation Adequacy

## 7.1 From Disclosure to Assessment

Section 6 established what transparency documentation must do: enable evaluators across the community of legitimate positions—cognitivist and non-cognitivist, formal and particularist—to trace intellectual contributions to human understanding and direction. The framework specifies what must be disclosed and why. This section addresses the complementary question: how should the scholarly community assess whether what is disclosed is adequate?

The question is not trivial. Documentation adequacy is not self-certifying. An author can produce five well-labelled supplementary files that, on examination, do not enable tracing. Conversely, documentation that initially appears spare may, on reading, provide what evaluators need. Assessment is required—and the community needs a shared approach to conducting it.

The original version of this paper proposed a reproduction test: a second reviewer would attempt to regenerate the intellectual contribution from the disclosed inputs, checking whether documented prompts and guidance were sufficient to produce work of comparable character. That test has been rejected for reasons established in Section 6. AI systems are non-deterministic; "reproduction" underdetermines the relevant adequacy question; and what must be traced is human understanding and direction, not a prompt-to-output mapping. The standard is documentation adequacy—does the disclosed record enable tracing assessment?—not reproduction success.

## 7.2 The Organizing Question

SP-3, the documentation account, is the primary site of the tracing claim. Its organizing question is: does this documentation adequately show how the intellectual trajectory of this work traces to human understanding and direction? SP-3 makes the author's case for adequacy; SP-4 provides the primary substance against which that case is assessed.

Documentation is adequate when it enables evaluators to answer three questions.

*Attribution*: Can evaluators locate the human contribution? Not in the trivial sense of confirming that a human was involved, but in the substantive sense of identifying where human judgment operated—what directions were set, what choices made, where the work departed from or redirected AI-generated material and why. Attribution is epistemic, not merely ethical: it is required because evaluators whose quality criteria are constitutively process-dependent cannot conduct their assessments without it, as Section 6.1 established.

*Intellectual trajectory*: Can evaluators follow how the work developed? Process documentation should reveal not just where the author arrived but how—the sequence of questions posed, the moments of revision and redirection, the emergence and testing of key ideas. A documentation account that presents only a polished outcome, without the intellectual history that produced it, falls short of adequacy regardless of its length or apparent thoroughness.

*Understanding and endorsement*: Does the documentation give reason to believe the author understood and endorsed the intellectual contributions they are presenting? This is the tracing condition's core demand. The documentation should make visible the evidence for this: corrections the author made to AI outputs, places where authorial judgment overrode AI suggestion, demonstrated capacity to explain what the argument does and defend it against objection. The goal is not proof—tracing does not require reconstruction of interior mental states—but reasonable grounds for attributing the intellectual contribution to the author's understanding.

## 7.3 A Dual Assessment Structure

The dual-reviewer architecture from Section 6.2 transfers to the documentation-adequacy model with modification.

*Quality assessment* proceeds as in standard peer review: evaluation of argument strength, conceptual clarity, originality, and scholarly rigor using the evaluator's own criteria. This assessment reads the submitted article. It does not require examination of supplementary materials—though evaluators whose quality criteria are constitutively process-dependent may find the documentation relevant to their assessment in ways discussed in Section 6.1.

*Documentation adequacy assessment* examines whether the tracing condition is satisfied. The assessor reads SP-1 (what the author declares about AI use), SP-2 (the navigation document enabling access to the documentation system), SP-3 (the documentation account and its adequacy argument), and, as needed, SP-4 (the underlying process materials). The assessment turns on the organizing question above: does this record enable attribution, trajectory-following, and reasonable inference to understanding-and-endorsement?

These assessments address distinct questions and should not be conflated. Work with strong philosophical quality may have inadequate documentation—the arguments are compelling, but the intellectual trajectory is opaque. Work with exemplary documentation may have weak arguments. These are separate failures, and evaluative judgment must weigh both while keeping them distinct. Philosophical quality cannot paper over documentation failures; documentation thoroughness cannot compensate for weak arguments.

## 7.4 Epistemic Norms for Assessment

The good faith orientation from Section 5 shapes the posture appropriate to documentation assessment. Assessment is not adversarial verification but epistemic inquiry: the question is whether the record enables the assessor to understand how this work came to be, not whether the author can be caught in inconsistency. This orientation is not naivety—it reflects both the self-selection effects of communities organized around transparency and the limits of what external verification can accomplish for genuinely internal epistemic processes. The ecological validity requirement from Section 5 is equally relevant here: assessment norms should remain sensitive to the fact that documentation emerges from real scholarly work rather than from compliance procedures, and should be calibrated accordingly.

Calibration matters. The depth of documentation review should be proportional to the complexity and significance of the claimed contribution. Work claiming that AI generated a central philosophical insight requires more sustained tracing than work claiming AI assisted with structuring a well-understood argument. The assessor's role is to probe proportionally—to follow the documentation as far as the claimed contribution requires, not to verify exhaustively.

Documentation assessment is itself a learning practice. Early assessors are necessarily improvising, working without established community norms for what adequate documentation looks like across different types of philosophical work. Norms can only develop through accumulated experience. Assessors who articulate what they found adequate or inadequate, and why, contribute to the community knowledge that Section 6.3 identifies as the framework's long-run goal. Engaged assessment reports—ones that take the documentation seriously rather than approving or rejecting in summary—accelerate community learning in ways that a purely accept-or-reject verdict cannot.

The self-exemplification of this article creates an immediate opportunity. The supplementary materials (SP-1 through SP-5) represent one implementation of the framework's requirements. Whether they are adequate—whether SP-3's tracing claim is supported by SP-4's underlying materials, whether the intellectual trajectory is legible, whether the author's understanding and endorsement are evidenced—is precisely the question this article invites the community to address. The framework provides its own first test case.
