# Section 3: Why Engage Transparently with AI-Assisted Ethics Research?

The practice of ethics research is changing. Scholars increasingly work with AI systems in ways that were impossible a decade ago—drafting arguments through iterative dialogue, testing objections against synthetic interlocutors, refining formulations across hundreds of exchanges. It is happening now, across institutions and subdisciplines, with varying degrees of visibility.

This raises a natural question: does AI assistance change ethics research *continuously* or *discontinuously*? Is it like the shift from typewriters to word processors—a change in the tools of production that leaves the essential activity untouched? Or is it something more fundamental—a transformation in what ethical inquiry actually is?

We cannot answer this question directly. The difficulty is not merely empirical but conceptual. Whether AI assistance changes ethical inquiry depends on what features of that inquiry are *constitutive* versus merely *regulative*—what defines the activity as opposed to what merely governs how it is conducted. And on this, there is no neutral ground.

## The Nature-of-Activities Problem

A parallel difficulty arises in debates about cognitive enhancement. Santoni de Sio, Faber, Savulescu, and Vincent (2016) observe that evaluating whether an enhancement "changes" an activity requires distinguishing constitutive rules from regulative ones. Constitutive rules define what an activity is; regulative rules merely govern how it is performed. Running a marathon on roller-skates is not running a marathon—the prohibition on wheels is constitutive of the event. But the ninety-minute duration of football games is arbitrary; altering it would change the experience without changing the fundamental nature of the sport.

The challenge is that most rules "fall somewhere between these two extremes. They are neither as vital as the ban on wheels in marathons nor as arbitrary as allocating 90 minutes to football games" (Santoni de Sio et al., 2016, p. 34). Where a given rule falls on this spectrum is often contested rather than obvious.

The same framework applies to ethical inquiry. Is unaided cognition constitutive of ethical work, or merely regulative? Is the process of reaching moral conclusions through one's own reasoning—attending to relevant considerations, exercising moral sensitivity, making judgments under uncertainty—part of what makes an activity ethical inquiry? Or could ethics research be conducted through iterative prompting and refinement, with the human contribution lying in the questions posed, the directions pursued, and the endorsement of outputs? These are not empirical questions admitting of neutral answers. They are contested questions about what ethical inquiry essentially is.

## Ethical Inquiry as Essentially Contested

The problem runs deeper still. "Ethical inquiry" is what Gallie (1956) termed an *essentially contested concept*—a concept whose proper application is itself the subject of ongoing dispute among competent practitioners. What counts as "doing ethics" is not fixed by stipulation or determined by criteria external to the practice. It is itself an ethical and philosophical question, and one on which practitioners characteristically disagree.

Practitioners disagree not only about first-order moral questions but about what methods are appropriate (reflective equilibrium, casuistry, principlism, particularism), what epistemic standards apply, and what purposes ethical reasoning serves. The depth of this contestation is illustrated by one of the most fundamental and unresolved debates in metaethics: whether moral claims are truth-apt at all. Whether ethical inquiry is in the business of tracking mind-independent moral facts, or whether it serves expressive, constructive, or social coordination functions—this question remains genuinely open (see Enoch 2011; Shafer-Landau 2003 for realist positions; Gibbard 1990; Blackburn 1993 for expressivist alternatives). It is disputed not merely at the margins but at the core of what ethics is.

The consequence is that we cannot settle whether AI fundamentally changes ethical inquiry without first settling what ethical inquiry is. But settling this would require resolving disputes that are constitutive of the discipline itself. The question "Does AI change ethics research?" presupposes an answer to a question that admits of no neutral adjudication.

## Why Output-Evaluation Fails in Ethics

It might seem that this conceptual difficulty does not matter for practical assessment. Whatever the constitutive structure of ethical inquiry turns out to be, surely we can evaluate AI-assisted outputs on their merits: Are the arguments valid? Are the conclusions defensible? Are the relevant moral considerations identified and weighed appropriately? This is the cognitivist objection: if ethics tracks truth, evaluate the outputs. A sound argument is sound regardless of how it was produced.

This objection deserves a serious reply rather than circumvention.

The cognitivist objection assumes that output-evaluation criteria are settled—that evaluators can agree on what makes an ethical argument good, and simply apply those criteria to whatever is produced. But this assumption is precisely what the essential contestedness of ethical inquiry undermines. Evaluators assess ethical outputs against background conceptions of what ethical inquiry is *for*, what methods it legitimately employs, and what competencies it requires. These background conceptions are themselves contested. There is no neutral, procedure-independent standpoint from which to "just evaluate" ethical outputs. The cognitivist objection borrows its force from a metaethical presupposition—that ethics tracks truth and that output-evaluation criteria are therefore settled—that is itself a paradigmatic instance of the essential contestedness it would need to overcome. The objection is question-begging: it assumes what is most disputed.

A qualification is necessary. The argument does not establish that process information is required for every ethical argument. For simple applied ethics reasoning with clear premises and valid inferences, output-evaluation may suffice. The claim is restricted to complex work involving contested methods, irreducible judgment, and genuine philosophical insight—precisely where AI assistance is most consequential, and where AI systems can produce outputs satisfying surface criteria without the understanding those criteria are meant to track.

## From Answer to Tracking

If the question of what ethical inquiry is cannot be answered—and if output-evaluation cannot substitute for understanding what AI assistance does to the activity—what can be done?

We can *track what ethics research is becoming*. Rather than presupposing that AI either does or does not transform ethical inquiry, we can create conditions under which the scholarly community can observe, compare, and evaluate different modes of ethical production. We can enable informed meta-ethical discussion about the changes underway, without prejudging the answer.

The uncertainty compounds when we consider the trajectory of AI-assisted practice itself. Initially, the focus falls on *prompting*—formulating queries that elicit useful outputs. Practice is already evolving toward *steering*—iteratively guiding AI systems through complex argumentative terrain, redirecting when lines of reasoning falter, synthesizing outputs across extended exchanges. Perhaps researchers will eventually become *architecture builders*—designing intellectual structures that AI systems populate with specific content. We do not know what the salient human contribution will become.

This compounds the original uncertainty. We face not only the contested question "what is ethical inquiry?" but also the open question "what will the relevant intellectual skills be under AI assistance?" If we cannot anticipate whether the salient human contribution will involve prompting, steering, architectural design, or something not yet imagined, we certainly cannot prejudge whether that contribution preserves or transforms the nature of ethical work.

## The Requirement of Visibility

Tracking requires visibility. If AI-assisted ethics research is produced opaquely—if the processes by which arguments are developed, refined, and selected remain hidden—then the scholarly community has no basis for assessing what is happening to ethical practice. Scholars cannot compare modes of production they cannot observe. They cannot evaluate transformations they cannot document.

This frames the practical proposals developed in subsequent sections. The meaningful human control framework (Section 6) operationalizes what it means for ethical work to remain attributable to human intellectual agency. Epistemic integrity requirements ensure that claims produced under AI assistance can be assessed for reliability. But both are instrumental to a higher goal: enabling the scholarly community to track, assess, and deliberate about what ethics research is becoming.

## The Stakes

Practices evolve; disciplines transform; what counts as legitimate method shifts over time. The question is whether that change will be *visible* enough for the community to understand it, *documented* enough for meaningful comparison across different modes of ethical production, and *attributable* enough for intellectual responsibility to be maintained. The transparency requirements proposed in this paper are designed to make that possible. They do not presuppose an answer to the question of what ethical inquiry is. They create the conditions under which that question can be productively addressed.
