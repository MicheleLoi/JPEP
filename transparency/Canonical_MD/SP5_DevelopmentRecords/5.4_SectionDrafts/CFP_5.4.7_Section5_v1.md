# 5. Conditions for Adequate Transparency

Section 2 identified the failure of compliance-based disclosure: four mechanisms—definitional flexibility, temporal discounting, comparative framing, and strategic vagueness—allow systematic underreporting without dishonesty, because current mandates specify *that* AI assistance must be reported without specifying what constitutes adequate reporting. The failure is structural. Addressing it requires prior philosophical work: specifying what transparency is *for*, and then identifying the conditions any framework pursuing those purposes must satisfy.

Three such conditions emerge from the diagnostic analysis. First, the framework must be *ecologically valid*—its requirements must work in actual scholarly practice, not merely satisfy a formal compliance standard. Second, it must be *oriented toward good faith*—designed for scholars committed to transparency rather than primarily aimed at catching evasion. Third, it must create a *cost structure* that makes honest, comprehensive disclosure both easier than strategic minimization and credibly differentiated from nominal compliance. Current disclosure mandates fail all three conditions. Any framework that meets them will have addressed the structural failure Section 2 identifies.

## 5.1 Ecological Validity

The first condition — ecological validity — concerns the fit between transparency requirements and the actual conditions of scholarly practice. A requirement is ecologically valid when honest compliance with it is the natural outcome of doing the work it is meant to document: when documentation arises from the research process itself rather than being reconstructed from it. Current disclosure mandates lack this property. Scholars are required to report AI use in formats designed for other purposes — acknowledgments sections, journal submission checklists — that were not designed to capture process information. Disclosure is appended to an existing scholarly workflow rather than integrated into it, ensuring that documentation is produced retrospectively, from memory, under submission deadline pressure. The four mechanisms from Section 2 are structurally enabled by this arrangement — each exploits the interpretive latitude that retrospective, open-ended characterization creates.

The positive principle, then, is that a transparency framework satisfies ecological validity when its documentation requirements emerge naturally from the process of genuine AI-assisted work, rather than demanding additional reconstructive effort after the fact.

## 5.2 Good Faith Orientation

Current mandates are adversarially structured: they require transparency under conditions where transparency is penalized. Scholars asked to disclose substantively must act against their immediate professional interests to comply, and the four mechanisms identified in Section 2 ensure that the resulting underreporting operates within genuine ambiguity rather than requiring dishonesty. Adding surveillance and detection machinery compounds the problem rather than solving it. Surveillance bureaucracy burdens honest scholars already paying the costs of genuine compliance, while arms races between gaming strategies and detection countermeasures favor sophisticated actors over conscientious ones. As Strathern (1997) observed of British university audit systems, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — precisely the dynamic that adversarially designed disclosure requirements reproduce.

A transparency framework adequate to this problem begins from a different diagnostic starting point. The mechanisms documented in Section 2 operate within legitimate interpretive latitude that current mandates themselves create; closing those ambiguities requires philosophical specification, not surveillance. Requirements that scholars committed to transparency find reasonable, proportionate, and genuinely useful will generate more and better documentation than requirements designed to catch evasion. This is not naivety about human behavior — it is recognition that the design of the requirement determines the quality of the response. Accountability follows from specificity: requirements that leave minimal interpretive latitude make substantive compliance the path of least resistance rather than an act of professional self-sacrifice. The second condition, then, is good faith orientation: an adequate transparency framework must be designed for scholars willing to comply honestly, structuring requirements so that genuine compliance is the natural and least costly response to well-specified obligations.

## 5.3 Cost Structure and Costly Signaling

A third failure of compliance-based mandates is false equivalence. A single sentence — "AI tools were used in preparation of this manuscript" — satisfies the same formal requirement as comprehensive process documentation. No differentiation exists between nominal and substantive compliance, and without such differentiation the evidentiary value of disclosure collapses: disclosure becomes a formal box to tick rather than information that enables assessment.

An adequate transparency framework must therefore create a cost structure in which comprehensive documentation is both the natural output of genuine AI-assisted work and credibly different from minimal disclosure. As Mercier (2020) argues in *Not Born Yesterday*, people assess communication for trustworthiness partly through cost considerations: signals that are expensive to produce prove harder to fake and thus more credible. Comprehensive process documentation functions as precisely this kind of signal. The burden of producing it marks submissions as genuine achievements.

This asymmetry is not absolute; AI tools can themselves assist in generating plausible-seeming documentation. But documentation specific enough to serve genuine epistemic functions is roughly as expensive to fabricate as to produce authentically, and the cost differential reduces the attractiveness of strategic minimization through design rather than moral exhortation. The third condition, then, is costly signaling: an adequate framework must ensure that comprehensive, honest documentation is a credible signal of genuine scholarly engagement — one that carries epistemic weight precisely because it cannot be generated cheaply.

## 5.4 From Conditions to Framework

Together, these three conditions—ecological validity, good faith orientation, and a cost structure producing meaningful differentiation—specify what compliance-based mandates lack and what an adequate transparency framework must provide. They are not independent desiderata but mutually reinforcing: a framework ecologically valid for honest scholarly practice will naturally generate the comprehensive documentation that differentiates substantive from nominal compliance; a framework designed for good faith participants will produce requirements that honest scholars find reasonable to meet. The conditions converge on the same design space.

Taken jointly, they also entail a further consequence: documentation produced under such a framework cannot function as raw disclosure to be taken at face value, but must constitute a defended claim about its own adequacy — one that the author is responsible for justifying and that others can assess.

The framework developed in Section 6 is designed to satisfy all three. Its documentation requirements are process-grounded rather than retrospective, designed for willing practitioners rather than for potential evaders, and structured so that comprehensive honest documentation emerges naturally from genuine AI-assisted work while remaining credibly differentiated from nominal compliance. What follows specifies how.
