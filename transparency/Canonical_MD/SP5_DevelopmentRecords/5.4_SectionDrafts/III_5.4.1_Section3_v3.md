# Section 3: Why Engage with AI-Assisted Scholarship?

The practice of philosophy is changing. Scholars increasingly work with AI systems in ways that were impossible a decade ago—drafting arguments through iterative dialogue, testing objections against synthetic interlocutors, refining formulations across hundreds of exchanges. It is happening now, across institutions and subdisciplines, with varying degrees of visibility.

This raises a natural question: does AI assistance change philosophy *continuously* or *discontinuously*? Is it like the shift from typewriters to word processors—a change in the tools of production that leaves the essential activity untouched? Or is it like something more fundamental—a transformation in what philosophical work actually is?

We cannot answer this question directly. The difficulty is not merely empirical but conceptual. Whether AI assistance changes philosophy depends on what features of philosophy are *constitutive* versus merely *regulative*—what defines the activity as opposed to what merely governs how it is conducted. And on this, there is no neutral ground.

## The Nature-of-Activities Problem

A parallel difficulty arises in debates about cognitive enhancement. Santoni de Sio, Faber, Savulescu, and Vincent (2016) observe that evaluating whether an enhancement "changes" an activity requires distinguishing constitutive rules from regulative ones. Constitutive rules define what an activity is; regulative rules merely govern how it is performed. Running a marathon on roller-skates is not running a marathon—the prohibition on wheels is constitutive of the event. But the ninety-minute duration of football games is arbitrary; altering it would change the experience without changing the fundamental nature of the sport.

The challenge is that most rules "fall somewhere between these two extremes. They are neither as vital as the ban on wheels in marathons nor as arbitrary as allocating 90 minutes to football games" (Santoni de Sio et al., 2016, p. 34). Where a given rule falls on this spectrum is often contested rather than obvious.

The same framework applies to philosophy. Is unaided cognition constitutive of philosophical work, or merely regulative? Is writing one's own sentences—selecting each word, constructing each inference—part of what makes an activity philosophical? Or could philosophy be conducted through prompt engineering and iterative refinement, with the human contribution lying in the questions posed, the directions pursued, and the judgments of quality rendered? These are not empirical questions admitting of neutral answers. They are contested questions about what philosophy essentially is.

## Philosophy as Essentially Contested

The problem runs deeper still. "Philosophy" is what Gallie (1956) termed an *essentially contested concept*—a concept whose proper application is itself the subject of ongoing dispute among competent practitioners. What counts as "doing philosophy" is not fixed by stipulation or determined by objective criteria external to the practice. It is itself a philosophical question, and one on which philosophers characteristically disagree.

The consequence is that we cannot settle whether AI fundamentally changes philosophy without first settling what philosophy is. But settling what philosophy is would require resolving disputes that are constitutive of the discipline itself—disputes that show no prospect of resolution and may be ineliminable features of philosophical practice. The question "Does AI change philosophy?" presupposes an answer to a question that admits of no neutral adjudication.

## From Answer to Tracking

If the question cannot be answered, what can be done?

We can *track what philosophy is becoming*. Rather than presupposing that AI either does or does not transform philosophical practice, we can create conditions under which the community can observe, compare, and evaluate different modes of philosophical production. We can enable informed meta-philosophical discussion about the changes underway, without prejudging the answer.

The uncertainty compounds when we consider the trajectory of AI-assisted practice itself. Initially, the focus falls on *prompting*—formulating queries that elicit useful outputs. But practice is already evolving toward what might be called *steering*—iteratively guiding AI systems through complex argumentative terrain, redirecting when lines of reasoning falter, synthesizing outputs across extended exchanges. Perhaps philosophers will eventually become *architecture builders*—designing intellectual structures that AI systems populate with specific content. We do not know what the salient human contribution will become.

This compounds the uncertainty. We face not only the contested question "what is philosophy?" but also the open question "what will the relevant philosophical skills be under AI assistance?" If we cannot anticipate whether the salient human contribution will involve prompting, steering, architectural design, or something not yet imagined, we certainly cannot prejudge whether that contribution preserves or transforms the nature of philosophical work.

## The Requirement of Visibility

Tracking requires visibility. If AI-assisted work is produced opaquely—if the processes by which arguments are developed, refined, and selected remain hidden—then the community has no basis for assessing what is happening to philosophical practice. Scholars cannot compare modes of production they cannot observe. They cannot evaluate transformations they cannot document.

This frames the practical proposals developed in subsequent sections. The meaningful human control framework (Section 6) operationalizes what it means for philosophical work to remain attributable to human intellectual agency. Epistemic integrity requirements—verification, replicability—ensure that claims produced under AI assistance can be assessed for reliability. But both are instrumental to a higher goal: enabling the scholarly community to track, assess, and deliberate about what philosophy is becoming.

## The Stakes

Practices evolve; disciplines transform; what counts as legitimate method shifts over time. The question is whether the change will be *visible* enough for the community to understand it, *documented* enough for meaningful comparison across different modes of production, and *attributable* enough for intellectual responsibility to be maintained. The transparency requirements proposed in this paper are designed to make that possible. They do not presuppose an answer to the question of what philosophy is. They create conditions under which the question can be productively addressed.
