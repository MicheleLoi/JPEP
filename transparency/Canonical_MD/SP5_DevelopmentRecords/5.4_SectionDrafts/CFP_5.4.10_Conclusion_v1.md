# 8. Conclusion

This paper has argued that the question of AI's role in ethics research cannot be settled by evaluating outputs against agreed standards, because the standards themselves are essentially contested. The contestation operates at two levels: we disagree about what ethical inquiry *is* and about what *doing* it requires of the inquirer. Both levels converge on the same practical requirement: comprehensive process documentation that enables the full community of legitimate evaluators to assess AI-assisted philosophical work on their own terms.

The convergence is structural. The conditions enabling tracking are the same conditions enabling authentic self-expression under AI assistance, because both flow from essential contestedness. Opacity defeats both simultaneously. The tracing condition — requiring that the directing person understood and endorses what was produced — operationalizes both the epistemic standard for tracking and the authenticity standard the philosophical tradition demands.

A framework satisfying both levels is more robust than one satisfying either alone. Its justification does not depend on resolving either level of contestation. Whether the reader holds that only argument quality matters or that the author's intellectual engagement is constitutive, the framework provides what they need. No legitimate evaluative tradition can dismiss the requirement without begging a question that remains genuinely open.

The paper's documentation apparatus — SP-1 through SP-5 — functions simultaneously as tracking instrument and as philosophical self-expression: a record of what the author chose to investigate, where they followed the AI, where they overrode it.

This self-exemplification requires honest acknowledgment. The early phase was documented retrospectively, under the pressures Section 2 identifies. The later phase, under a prospective protocol, produced a fuller record. The three criteria of Section 7 were tested against the paper's own record and held — but the test revealed that trajectory documentation degrades unless contemporaneous. We built the case for documentation requirements while discovering, through building, what those requirements need to specify.

The dual assessment structure of Section 7 provides a starting architecture; norms governing documentation assessment can only develop through practice.

What we have proposed is modest in scope: a single transparency framework, demonstrated through a single case. The implication is that the epistemic conditions of philosophical practice are changing, and that the discipline's commitment to methodological self-consciousness requires extending to this new dimension. There is a risk that documentation requirements could become audit machinery — bureaucratic compliance replacing genuine engagement. The good faith and ecological validity conditions resist this, but the tension must be monitored. Documentation is what philosophy's own contested nature demands when the conditions of production change — provided it remains intellectual self-examination rather than compliance exercise.
