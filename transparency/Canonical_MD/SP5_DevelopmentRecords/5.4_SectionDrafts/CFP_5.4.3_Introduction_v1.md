# 1. Introduction

The use of artificial intelligence in academic work has generated substantial discussion across two domains. In education, a focus of concern has emerged: AI tools are held to threaten cognitive development, undermine assessment validity, and erode the trust that sustains pedagogical relationships (Jollimore 2025; Berg & Robbins 2024). In scientific research more broadly, a growing literature addresses disclosure norms, authorship attribution, and the reliability of AI-assisted outputs (Hosseini et al. 2023; Van Woudenberg et al. 2024). Yet one domain remains almost entirely unexamined: the use of AI tools in ethics research specifically. This gap is not incidental. Ethics is where the question is hardest — not because ethical inquiry is generically more demanding, but because what constitutes ethical inquiry, its methods, its epistemic structure, its purpose, is itself fundamentally disputed. Any serious account of AI's role in ethics research must begin from this fact.

Contemporary journal policies require disclosure of AI assistance (COPE Council 2024; Elsevier 2023; ACM 2025; Science 2023), a norm now adopted by the majority of major journals (Lund & Naheem 2023). Yet these policies operate without philosophical clarity about what transparency is meant to serve. Is there a principled case for process transparency in ethics research at all? Section 2 provides a structural analysis of the disclosure environment. What concerns us here is the prior question.

The most direct challenge takes a familiar form. If ethics is in the business of tracking moral truth, then what matters is whether the arguments produced are sound, the conclusions defensible, the reasoning valid. A sound argument is sound regardless of how it was produced: by solitary reflection, by collaborative conversation, or by iterative prompting of a language model. Process transparency confuses the context of discovery with the context of justification. Evaluate the outputs.

This objection deserves a serious reply rather than circumvention. The reply has two components and a qualification.

The first component is that evaluation in ethics is already partly process-dependent. There is no moral truth-meter. Assessing a philosophical argument requires attending to whether the right considerations were identified and the right sensitivities exercised, not merely whether the inference is valid. A cognitivist might dismiss this as a contingent limitation of current practice. The second component shows why it is not contingent.

"Ethical inquiry" is, in W. B. Gallie's (1956) sense, an essentially contested concept: appraisive, internally complex, variously describable by its practitioners, open to ongoing revision, and subject to persistent reasonable disagreement among competent users. Practitioners disagree not only about first-order moral questions but about what methods are appropriate, what epistemic standards apply, and what purposes ethical reasoning serves. The depth of this contestation is illustrated by the cognitivism/non-cognitivism dispute — one of the most fundamental and unresolved debates in metaethics. Whether moral claims are truth-apt, whether moral predicates function as genuine descriptions, whether ethical inquiry tracks mind-independent moral facts — these questions remain genuinely open (see Enoch 2011; Shafer-Landau 2003 for realist positions; Gibbard 1990; Blackburn 1993 for expressivist alternatives). The cognitivist objection to process transparency presupposes that ethics tracks truth and that output-evaluation criteria are therefore settled. But this presupposition is precisely what is most contested. The objection is question-begging: it borrows its force from a metaethical commitment that is itself a paradigmatic instance of the essential contestedness it would need to overcome.

A qualification is necessary. This argument does not establish that process information is required for every ethical argument. For simple applied ethics reasoning with clear premises and valid inferences, output-evaluation may suffice. The claim is restricted to complex philosophical work involving contested methods, irreducible judgment, and genuine philosophical insight — precisely where AI assistance is most consequential, and where AI systems can produce outputs satisfying surface criteria without the understanding those criteria are meant to track.

If output-evaluation in ethics is irreducibly process-dependent, and if the criteria for evaluation are themselves contested, then the achievable goal for transparency requirements is not evaluation of outputs against agreed standards but *tracking*: monitoring what ethics research is becoming under AI assistance and accumulating the evidentiary record that makes future normative judgments possible. Tracking is prior to evaluation. It creates the visibility without which damage cannot be detected and the evidence base without which normative claims cannot be supported. The charge of quietism — that tracking substitutes for normative judgment — gets the priority relation backwards: tracking is what makes normative judgment possible.

Tracking requires more than a disclosure mandate. It requires a philosophically specified transparency framework that identifies what information must be preserved, in what form, and against what standards of adequacy. Current disclosure policies establish that AI use must be reported; they do not specify what transparency is *for* or what constitutes adequate documentation. This silence is a philosophical problem, not a gap in regulatory design.

We argue for a transparency framework grounded in Meaningful Human Control (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018) and operationalized through a documentation-adequacy model: a specification of what AI-assisted ethics research must document, what that documentation must demonstrate, and what assessment of documentation adequacy requires. The paper is itself an instance of substantially AI-assisted ethics research conducted under the framework it describes. The transparency apparatus — supplementary packages SP-1 through SP-5, documenting AI involvement, decision rationale, and process records — is implemented in the work here presented. This self-exemplification is a methodological commitment: the framework's value cannot be fully assessed from a description; it must be shown in operation. We acknowledge that the present venue does not include specialized infrastructure for evaluating documentation packages of this scope; community assessment mechanisms for documentation adequacy remain to be developed. We also acknowledge that a transparency framework for essentially contested inquiry is itself a contested proposal — Section 6 treats the framework as a first iteration subject to revision rather than a settled standard.
