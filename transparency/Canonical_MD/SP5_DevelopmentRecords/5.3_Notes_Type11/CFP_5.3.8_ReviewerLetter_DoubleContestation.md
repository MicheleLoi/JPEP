# Reviewer Report

**Manuscript:** "Mandatory Transparency for AI-Assisted Ethics Research: A Documentation-Adequacy Framework"
**Venue:** *Philosophy & Technology* — Topical Collection: AI Tools in Ethics Research
**Recommendation:** Major revisions (revise and resubmit)

---

## Summary

The paper argues that transparency requirements for AI-assisted ethics research cannot be grounded in agreed output-evaluation criteria because "ethical inquiry" is an essentially contested concept. It identifies two levels of contestation — meta-ethical (what ethical inquiry is) and ethical (what doing it requires of the inquirer) — and argues that both lead to the same practical requirement: comprehensive process documentation. The paper proposes a framework based on Meaningful Human Control (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven, 2018) and demonstrates it through its own transparency apparatus.

The central contribution — the "double contestation" argument — is genuinely novel and addresses a gap the CFP explicitly identifies: the near-absence of philosophical treatment of AI's role in ethics research specifically. The paper is relevant to several of the CFP's questions, particularly regarding risks, methodological implications, and the discovery/justification distinction.

---

## Strengths

**1. The double contestation is a real philosophical contribution.** The move from one level of essential contestedness (what ethical inquiry IS) to two (what doing it REQUIRES) is not merely additive. It significantly strengthens the paper's practical argument: a single framework serving two independent justificatory routes is more robust than one serving either alone. The argument that the tracing condition operationalizes both the epistemic standard for tracking and the authenticity standard is the paper's most original claim and is convincingly developed in §6.1.

**2. The ecumenical architecture is disciplined.** The paper never asserts the truth of the Socrates-Kierkegaard-Nietzsche tradition. It consistently argues that because we cannot settle whether this tradition is correct, we must enable its adherents to assess AI-assisted work on their own terms. This is philosophically careful and avoids a common trap in papers invoking continental or existentialist thought within analytic frameworks.

**3. The self-exemplification is handled honestly.** The Conclusion's acknowledgment that the paper's own transparency record is a partial instance — retrospective in its early phase, prospective later — is refreshing. The observation that trajectory documentation degrades unless contemporaneous is derived from the paper's own experience and genuinely strengthens the argument.

**4. The Abdulhai et al. (2026) integration is effective.** The stance-neutralization finding provides empirical support that is directly relevant to the non-cognitivist arm of the argument and is deployed precisely where it belongs (§7.2).

---

## Concerns Requiring Revision

**1. The authenticity argument needs sharper engagement with existing literature on AI and philosophical authorship.**

The paper invokes Socrates, Kierkegaard, and Nietzsche at length but engages minimally with the contemporary philosophical literature on authorship, creativity, and AI. There is a growing body of work on AI and creative authorship (e.g., Boden 2004 on computational creativity; Coeckelbergh 2020 on AI and imagination; Gaut 2010 on creativity and originality). The "romantic objection" and its defeat via architect/AARON parallels recapitulate moves that have been explored in aesthetics and philosophy of art. The paper would benefit from acknowledging this literature and positioning its contribution relative to it — even if the point is that the ethics-research context generates distinctive considerations.

The Cohen/AARON example, while apt, is presented without citation. McCorduck (1991) should be cited, or a more accessible recent source.

**2. The relationship between the tracking argument and the authenticity argument is clearer than the paper admits — but this creates a tension.**

The paper claims the two levels generate "independent" routes to the same requirement, and that the framework's robustness comes from not depending on resolving either contestation. But the authenticity route — Socrates, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche — is itself one of the contested positions within the meta-ethical contestation. The tracking argument is metaethically neutral; the authenticity argument is not neutral — it derives its force from one contested tradition. The paper is careful to frame the authenticity argument ecumenically ("because we cannot settle whether this tradition is correct..."), but a sceptical reader might object that Level 2 is parasitic on Level 1 rather than genuinely independent. If the only reason to care about authenticity requirements is that we cannot rule out the tradition that makes them constitutive, then the second level adds no new normative force beyond what the first already provides. The paper should address this potential objection explicitly. One possible response: Level 2 adds *specificity* — it tells us what documentation must be able to show (not just "process information" but specifically information bearing on the author's mode of engagement), and this specificity guides the framework's design in ways Level 1 alone does not.

**3. Section 3's treatment of the tradition is dense and could be more clearly signposted.**

The paragraph introducing Socrates, Kierkegaard, and Nietzsche is approximately 150 words of compressed philosophical history. For a *Philosophy & Technology* readership that may not have deep familiarity with *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* or *Beyond Good and Evil* §6, the philosophical claims need to be unpacked slightly more. The current presentation reads like a catalogue of positions rather than an argument. The Kierkegaard point in particular — "the how of relating to truth matters constitutively" — is doing significant philosophical work but is stated rather than argued. A sentence or two explaining *why* Kierkegaard thinks the how matters (the leap, the relationship between subjectivity and ethical commitment) would help readers who are not specialists in 19th-century European philosophy.

**4. §6.1 is too long relative to the rest of the paper.**

At approximately 1,050 words, §6.1 is now the longest single subsection in the paper. It carries the full weight of the authenticity argument — the romantic objection, its defeat through two analogies, the citation precedent, modes of engagement, opacity-as-imposture, convergence, and the MHC framework. This is a great deal of argumentative work for a single subsection. Consider whether the MHC framework discussion (currently at the end of §6.1) could be separated into its own subsection, which would also better match the paper's organizational logic: §6.1 establishes the requirement; a new §6.1b or §6.2 (with renumbering) operationalizes it.

**5. The paper's section numbering has a gap (3 → 5).**

This is presumably an artifact of adaptation from an earlier version. For submission, the sections should be renumbered consecutively.

**6. The Conclusion's closing sentence is effective but could acknowledge one more thing.**

"Documentation is not bureaucracy imposed on philosophy from outside. It is what philosophy's own contested nature demands when the conditions of philosophical production change." This is a strong closing. However, the paper does not address the possibility that documentation requirements, if institutionalized, could themselves become a bureaucratic burden that undermines the authentic engagement they are meant to preserve — a version of Strathern's (1997) audit-culture critique applied to the paper's own proposal. Section 5's good faith orientation partially addresses this, but the Conclusion should signal awareness of the tension.

---

## Minor Points

- The paper should clarify the section numbering scheme for readers (or simply renumber).
- The distinction between "tracking" and "tracing" in the MHC framework is crucial but could be flagged more clearly for readers unfamiliar with Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven. A parenthetical gloss at first use might help.
- Abdulhai et al. (2026) is cited as "et al." with a footnote promising full author list at copyedit. This should be completed before submission.
- The paper would benefit from an explicit statement of its word count and a note on supplementary materials, given the unusual scope of the transparency apparatus.

---

## Fit with the Topical Collection

The paper directly addresses several CFP questions: the discovery/justification distinction (rejected as inadequate, with a more sophisticated alternative proposed); risks of AI in ethics research (stance neutralization, loss of authentic engagement); and methodological implications (documentation as both tracking instrument and expression of philosophical identity). The focus on ethics research *specifically* — rather than AI in scholarship generally — is well-maintained throughout, and the paper's argument depends on features of ethics (essential contestedness, metaethical disagreement) that distinguish it from other fields. This is a strong fit for the collection.

---

## Summary Assessment

The paper makes a genuine philosophical contribution with the double contestation argument and its operationalization through the tracing condition. The self-exemplification is bold and largely convincing. The main weaknesses are: (a) insufficient engagement with existing literature on AI, creativity, and authorship; (b) the need to address the parasitism objection to Level 2's independence; and (c) structural/proportionality issues in §6.1. These are addressable through revision. The paper deserves the opportunity to revise and resubmit.
