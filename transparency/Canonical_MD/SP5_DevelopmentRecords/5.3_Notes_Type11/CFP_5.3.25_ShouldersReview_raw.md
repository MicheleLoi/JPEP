# Peer Review Report

## Peer Review Summary

### General Impression
This paper addresses a genuine and underexamined problem: what transparency norms should govern AI-assisted ethics research specifically, and what philosophical justification supports them. The central argument — that ethical inquiry is an essentially contested concept, that output-evaluation criteria are therefore not settled, and that comprehensive process documentation is thus required for tracking and self-expressive purposes — is original and substantive. The operationalization via Meaningful Human Control is theoretically ambitious. However, the manuscript is submitted in a condition that is not ready for peer review, let alone publication, and the problems are structural rather than cosmetic.

### Strengths
The essentially-contested-concept argument in Section 3 is the paper's most distinctive contribution and is genuinely well-developed. The identification of four mechanisms producing systematic underreporting (Section 2) is analytically sharp and practically useful. The self-exemplifying methodology is an intellectually honest and philosophically fitting choice for this topic.

### Key Issues

**Submission completeness.** The abstract is entirely absent [15, 16]. The supplementary packages SP-1 through SP-5, which are foundational to the self-exemplification claim, are not submitted [3, 17]. Without them, the paper's central methodological contribution cannot be evaluated at all.

**Structural incoherence.** Section numbering is pervasively inconsistent — dual-numbering throughout, Section 4 missing, roadmap mismatched to body [8, 24]. The bibliography contains unpublished editorial workflow notes, verification flags, and project-management metadata that must be removed entirely before any submission [14, 18].

**Underdeveloped theoretical moves.** The transfer of MHC from autonomous weapons to scholarly authorship is asserted rather than argued [7, 23]. The question-begging charge against the cognitivist objection is itself vulnerable to a symmetric counter-charge that the paper does not address [5, 19]. The Sartrean bad faith application is underspecified for the normative work it is asked to do [30].

**Evidentiary problems.** The Abdulhai et al. (2026) preprint is cited as though establishing firm findings; study design, operationalization, and the preprint status require flagging, and the inference to ethics research specifically requires justification [1, 2, 25]. The costly-signaling argument is undermined by the very AI capacity being documented [20].

**Self-exemplification tension.** The paper discloses, in a brief concluding caveat, that its own early phases were documented retrospectively — the precise failure mode the ecological validity condition prohibits. This materially affects the proof-of-concept claim and must be addressed prominently, not noted in passing [27, 13].

**Missing and inconsistent citations.** Mercier (2020) and Strathern (1997) are cited but absent from the bibliography [11, 12]. The Santoni de Sio et al. (2016) chapter has conflicting editor attributions [9]. The Williams ground-projects citation likely points to the wrong work [31].

### Overall Assessment
The philosophical core of this paper is promising and worth developing, but the manuscript requires substantial revision before it can be assessed on its merits: the abstract must be written, the supplementary materials submitted, the numbering and bibliography cleaned, and several foundational arguments either strengthened or more honestly qualified.

## Comments

### 15. [major] — Technical Reviewer
> "Not yet drafted"

The abstract is entirely absent from this submission. An abstract is not merely a formal requirement: it performs an essential epistemic function, allowing readers, reviewers, and indexing services to assess the paper's scope, claims, and conclusions prior to engaging the full text. For a paper of this complexity — spanning metaethics, philosophy of action, research methodology, and practical framework specification — a working abstract is essential for reviewers to evaluate whether the framing accurately represents the contributions made. This must be drafted before submission.

### 19. [major] — Editorial Reviewer
> "The cognitivist objection presupposes that ethics tracks truth and that output-evaluation criteria are therefore settled — but this presupposition is precisely what is most contested in metaethics. The objection is question-begging. Section 3 develops this argument"

The charge of question-begging is made twice in the paper (here in the Introduction and again in Section 3.4) but requires more careful handling. The cognitivist objection does not straightforwardly 'presuppose' that metaethical disputes are settled — it assumes only that, given cognitivism, output evaluation suffices. The author's counter-argument shows that this assumption is contested, not that the cognitivist is illicitly assuming their conclusion within the argument. More precisely, the cognitivist is offering a conditional: IF cognitivism is true, output evaluation suffices. The author needs to explain why we should not simply reason on each metaethical hypothesis separately, rather than concluding that the objection itself is question-begging. The argument as stated moves too quickly and may conflate 'contested assumption' with 'question-begging'.

### 4. [minor] — Technical Reviewer
> "the tradition from Socrates through Kierkegaard and Nietzsche maintains"

The philosophical lineage 'Socrates through Kierkegaard and Nietzsche' is invoked repeatedly as a unified tradition treating philosophical inquiry as constitutively tied to the inquirer's personal engagement. However, these thinkers differ substantially in their conceptions of the relationship between the inquirer and philosophical output — Socrates' dialogical method, Kierkegaard's pseudonymous authorship (itself a form of constructed philosophical persona), and Nietzsche's performative self-fashioning are not straightforwardly unified. The paper's argument would be strengthened by acknowledging these internal tensions rather than presenting the three as a homogeneous tradition. As it stands, the appeal to 'the tradition' risks an oversimplification that a specialist reader in the history of philosophy will notice.

### 7. [suggestion] — Technical Reviewer
> "We argue for a transparency framework grounded in Meaningful Human Control (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018) and operationalized through a documentation-adequacy model"

The transfer of the Meaningful Human Control (MHC) framework from autonomous weapons systems to AI-assisted scholarship is the paper's central theoretical move, yet the conditions under which this transfer is legitimate receive insufficient scrutiny. MHC was developed for systems where human operators have real-time decisional authority over lethal force — a context where the stakes, the reversibility of actions, and the nature of 'control' are radically different from iterative philosophical writing. The paper acknowledges the transfer ('the framework transfers structurally') but does not systematically examine which features of MHC are preserved and which are transformed in the scholarly context. A more rigorous treatment would identify the structural homologies and disanalogies explicitly.

### 17. [major] — Editorial Reviewer

The transparency apparatus — supplementary packages SP-1 through SP-5, documenting AI involvement, decision rationale, and process records — is implemented in the work here presented."

The supplementary packages SP-1 through SP-5, which are central to the paper's self-exemplifying methodology and the operationalisation of its core framework, are not included with the submission. The paper repeatedly invites readers and reviewers to assess these materials (e.g., 'the question this article invites the community to address' in Section 7), yet they are entirely absent. If the framework's value 'cannot be fully assessed from a description' but 'must be shown in operation', the submission is structurally incomplete without them. The author should either include the supplementary packages or clearly explain their current status and how readers are expected to assess the self-exemplification claim.

### 9. [minor] — Technical Reviewer

### 21. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "A requirement is ecologically valid when honest compliance is the natural outcome of doing the work it documents: when documentation arises from the research process itself rather than being reconstructed from it."

The concept of 'ecological validity' is used here in a non-standard way. In research methodology, ecological validity refers to the generalisability of findings to real-world settings. The author appears to mean something closer to 'process-embedded' or 'concurrent documentation' rather than the standard methodological meaning. Using established terminology with a stipulated redefinition risks confusion. The author should either use a different term (e.g., 'concurrent validity', 'process-embedded documentation') or explicitly flag the non-standard usage at first introduction.

### 12. [minor] — Technical Reviewer
> "As Strathern (1997) observed, “when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”"

Strathern (1997) is quoted in the body text but is absent from both the section-level reference list and the complete paper bibliography. The missing reference must be added. Additionally, this aphorism is commonly associated with both Strathern and Goodhart ('Goodhart's Law'); the paper should cite the specific Strathern work to avoid ambiguity and allow readers to verify the quotation.

### 26. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "In modular synthesis, the composer designs the system architecture — patching together oscillators, filters, and sequencers — rather than controlling every waveform; sonic properties emerge from module interactions in ways not fully predictable from individual settings. In computer-based generative art, the artist establishes abstract rules implemented by a computer such that the system is “partly responsible for coming up with the idea itself” (Boden & Edmonds, 2009, p. 138) — Harold Cohen’s AARON is the paradigmatic case."

The analogies to modular synthesis and generative art are illustrative but rest on a disanalogy that needs to be addressed. In both cases, the artist/composer designs the generative system from scratch — specifying its architecture and rules. In LLM-assisted writing, the author prompts a large, pre-existing, externally-trained system whose architecture they did not design and largely do not understand. This distinction is potentially significant for the self-expression and creative agency claims being made. The argument would be strengthened by acknowledging and responding to this difference rather than leaving the analogy unqualified.

### 23. [major] — Editorial Reviewer
> "Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) developed MHC for autonomous weapons systems, but the framework transfers structurally to AI-assisted scholarship."

The transfer of Meaningful Human Control from autonomous weapons to AI-assisted scholarship is the paper's central operationalising move, yet the claim that it 'transfers structurally' is made without systematic justification. Autonomous weapons contexts involve physical harm, military command chains, and immediate causal agency — very different from iterative scholarly authorship. The author should explicitly identify the structural features of MHC that are claimed to transfer (e.g., the tracking/tracing distinction), acknowledge the features that do not transfer, and argue for why the transferred elements are sufficient for the scholarly context. This is currently under-argued for such a foundational move.

### 10. [minor] — Technical Reviewer
> "systems whose actions and states are not traceable to relevant understanding and endorsing by some human person—no matter how intelligent and reason-responsive they may be—are not under meaningful human control"

The direct quotation from Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven (2018) is attributed with the locator '§6.2' (a section number) rather than a page number, while other direct quotations in the paper use page numbers (e.g., 'p. 34'). Citation style should be consistent throughout. More substantively, since this paper repurposes a framework developed for autonomous weapons for a very different domain (scholarship), reviewers and readers need a verifiable locator to confirm the quote is accurate and that the surrounding context supports the transfer of the framework as the author describes it.

### 29. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "We reject Standard 4: real workflows involve iterative refinement where “AI text” and “human text” blur. What matters is whether the intellectual trajectory is traceable to human understanding—which is what SP-4 captures."

The rejection of Lloyd's Standard 4 (intra-textual clarity) is dismissed in a single sentence without engaging with Lloyd's rationale for the standard. Given that Lloyd (2025) is a peer-reviewed work being explicitly engaged with, the author should explain what Standard 4 requires and why it was proposed, present the strongest case for it, and then argue specifically why the SP-4 tracing approach adequately serves the epistemic functions that Standard 4 was designed to achieve. The current dismissal is insufficiently argued.

### 28. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "Research communities organized around opacity face an adverse selection dynamic: as non-transparent AI use becomes widespread, the epistemic value of all undisclosed work diminishes collectively, while scholars committed to transparency bear individual costs. Communities organized around transparency invert this: they attract scholars motivated by the desire to learn from one another’s documented practice."

This claim about community dynamics is asserted without supporting evidence or argument. The claim that transparency communities 'invert' the adverse selection dynamic is not self-evidently true — it is at least equally plausible that they face a first-mover disadvantage, or attract only already-committed scholars rather than reshaping broader norms. The author should either engage with relevant literature on norm change in academic communities, or explicitly flag this as a hopeful conjecture rather than a demonstrated dynamic.

### 25. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "Recent empirical evidence underscores why these criteria are non-trivial. Abdulhai et al. (2026) find that LLM-assisted writing produces a 68.9% increase in stance neutralization—AI systematically erases the author’s evaluative commitments while increasing surface markers of expressiveness. On expressivist or sentimentalist accounts, a tool that neutralizes stance while preserving the appearance of engagement threatens ethical inquiry at its core."

This is the paper's only substantive piece of empirical evidence and it carries significant argumentative weight, but two issues arise. First, Abdulhai et al. (2026) is an unreviewed arXiv preprint; the author should flag this status and treat the findings with appropriate epistemic caution rather than citing them as established findings. Second, the inference from 'stance neutralization in LLM-assisted writing generally' to 'LLM-assisted ethics research specifically' requires justification — the study scope (genre, text type) should be stated, and the claim calibrated accordingly.

### 2. [major] — Technical Reviewer
> "LLM-assisted texts score higher on perceived quality metrics even as genuine evaluative content diminishes — output assessment alone cannot detect the loss that process documentation would reveal."

This claim directly follows the Abdulhai et al. citation but conflates two distinct empirical assertions: (a) that LLM-assisted texts score higher on perceived quality metrics, and (b) that 'genuine evaluative content diminishes'. Claim (b) in particular depends on a normative and philosophical definition of 'genuine evaluative content' that the empirical study cannot straightforwardly establish. The inference that 'output assessment alone cannot detect the loss' is presented as an empirical finding, but it is partly a normative claim dependent on accepting an expressivist or sentimentalist metaethics — the very contested position the paper argues for. The logical leap from a preprint's quantitative findings to this conclusion should be flagged as an inference requiring additional argument, not a straightforward empirical result.

### 27. [major] — Editorial Reviewer
> "The early phase was documented retrospectively, under the pressures Section 2 identifies. The later phase, under a prospective protocol, produced a fuller record. The three criteria of Section 7 were tested against the paper’s own record and held — but the test revealed that trajectory documentation degrades unless contemporaneous."

This is a critical self-disclosure that appears only in the final paragraph of the Conclusion. The paper's core ecological validity condition requires contemporaneous documentation, yet the author here discloses that the paper's own early phases were documented retrospectively — the precise failure mode the framework is designed to prevent. This tension materially affects whether the paper successfully demonstrates the framework it advocates. It should be addressed much earlier in the paper — ideally in Section 6 when the ecological validity condition is first introduced — not only acknowledged as a brief caveat in the conclusion.

### 22. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer

This is an important limitation that is mentioned only fleetingly in the final paragraph of the Conclusion. The risk of documentation requirements becoming compliance theatre is in fact a central tension for the entire framework — it bears on the good-faith orientation condition, the costly-signaling claim, and the self-exemplification methodology. It deserves treatment as a genuine limitation in a dedicated section or sub-section, not merely as a one-sentence caveat. The author should consider expanding this into a more substantive engagement with the limits and potential failure modes of the proposed framework.

### 1. [major] — Technical Reviewer

### 5. [minor] — Technical Reviewer
> "The cognitivist objection presupposes that ethics tracks truth and that output-evaluation criteria are therefore settled — but this presupposition is precisely what is most contested in metaethics. The objection is question-begging."

The charge that the cognitivist objection is 'question-begging' is itself methodologically questionable as deployed here. The paper argues: (1) output-evaluation presupposes cognitivism; (2) cognitivism is contested; therefore (3) output-evaluation begs the question. But the paper's own argument for process transparency equally presupposes that the expressivist/existentialist conception is a live option. A cognitivist could respond that the paper's argument for transparency begs the question in the other direction — by treating the personal/existential conception as sufficient grounds for mandatory documentation requirements that apply to everyone, including cognitivists. The paper should engage more carefully with this symmetry, as 'question-begging' is a strong logical charge that cuts in multiple directions here.

### 6. [suggestion] — Technical Reviewer
> "Research communities organized around opacity face an adverse selection dynamic: as non-transparent AI use becomes widespread, the epistemic value of all undisclosed work diminishes collectively, while scholars committed to transparency bear individual costs."

The adverse selection argument is intuitive but presented without formal support or citation. Adverse selection is a well-defined concept from economics/game theory with specific structural requirements (information asymmetry, market dynamics, etc.). The claim that opacity creates an adverse selection dynamic in academic publishing is plausible but requires either a more careful analytic development or citation of literature that has made this argument rigorously. As stated, the claim is asserted rather than demonstrated, and its precision ('adverse selection' specifically, rather than a simpler race-to-the-bottom framing) invites scrutiny the paper does not address.

### 20. [major] — Editorial Reviewer
> "As Mercier (2020) argues in _Not Born Yesterday_, signals expensive to produce prove harder to fake and thus more credible. Comprehensive documentation is such a signal. AI tools can generate plausible-seeming documentation, but documentation specific enough to serve genuine epistemic functions is roughly as expensive to fabricate as to produce authentically."

The costly-signaling claim requires more careful argument. The paper asserts that sufficiently specific documentation is 'roughly as expensive to fabricate as to produce authentically', but this is not obviously true. An actor with substantial AI assistance could use those same AI tools to generate detailed-seeming but fabricated process documentation at low cost. If AI assistance is the very thing being documented, the fabrication problem is not analogous to e.g. costly biological signals. The author needs to engage more seriously with this objection rather than asserting equivalence of fabrication and authentic production costs.

### 30. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "Silently treating the output-only view as exhaustive is not merely an unjustified assumption; it is what Sartre would recognize as _bad faith_ — a denial of freedom in the form of self-deception. The philosopher who uses AI to build philosophical architectures exercises genuine creative freedom. The bad faith lies not in the use but in treating that use as “just ordinary business” — denying that it raises questions requiring engagement with others. This is a refusal to acknowledge that one’s philosophical practice is free, and that this freedom entails accountability to those who conceive of philosophy differently."

The invocation of Sartrean bad faith is philosophically underspecified and risks overextension. Sartre's concept of bad faith involves self-deception about one's own freedom and facticity — a first-person psychological and ontological concept. Applying it as a normative charge against scholars who do not disclose AI use is a significant conceptual leap: is non-disclosure genuinely Sartrean self-deception, or rather epistemic negligence toward others? The author should either develop the Sartrean application more carefully with further textual support, or explicitly acknowledge this is a loose analogical use rather than a direct application of the concept.

### 31. [minor] — Editorial Reviewer
> "Bernard Williams’s notion of integrity specifies the social dimension of this accountability. Your _ground projects_ — the commitments constitutive of your identity as an agent — include your mode of philosophical inquiry: how you think, what methods you employ, how you relate to your own work (Williams, 1981). When that mode changes significantly through AI assistance, transparency is how you honor the coherence of those commitments before a community that has reason to care about them."

Williams's 'ground projects' concept was developed primarily in his contribution to Smart & Williams (1973), 'Utilitarianism: For and Against', not in 'Persons, Character and Morality' (1981). The author should verify the correct locus of the ground projects argument and update the citation accordingly. Additionally, the extension of ground projects to 'mode of philosophical inquiry' goes beyond Williams's original context of utilitarian demandingness; this extension should be explicitly flagged and defended rather than simply asserted.
