# Briefing: Brian D. Earp on AI-Assisted Scholarly Writing

## 1. Purpose and scope

This briefing maps Brian D. Earp's published corpus on AI-assisted academic authorship onto JPEP's argument about process transparency. It is deliberately constrained: only pieces for which **full body text was independently accessed and read** are treated as primary evidence; the wider corpus is recorded but explicitly marked **body-not-accessed**, with citation metadata only. The intent is to give future JPEP work (Phase 5 read-through, post-rejection reframings, possible EthIT-edition revisions) a citable map of where Earp's framework supplies useful adjacent material, where it operates on a different axis than JPEP's, and how JPEP's transparency principle relates to the authorship debate Earp's work is centred in — namely, by subsuming it. JPEP does not adjudicate authorship; it claims that the reader, equipped with process documentation, should be the one who adjudicates, and authorship is one of the contested questions a thick process disclosure enables the reader to settle for themselves.

A short attribution-revisionist counter to Earp's "provenance problem" claim is added at the end, drawing on Cordasco's peer-review post. The extension from peer-review function to authored-scholarship attribution is **explicitly JPEP's**, not Cordasco's — flagged as such.

## 2. Verified-evidence core (full body text read)

### 2.1 Earp, Porsdam Mann, Sawai & Wangmo (2026) — "Death, authorship, and generative AI — a call for commentaries"

**Citation.** Earp, B. D., Porsdam Mann, S., Sawai, T., & Wangmo, T. (2026). Death, authorship, and generative AI — a call for commentaries. *JME Practical Bioethics*, 2, e000046.
**Accessibility.** Full text read via Academia.edu mirror: [academia.edu/167307834](https://www.academia.edu/167307834).
**DOI status.** ⚠ The DOI `10.1136/jmepb-2025-000046` reportedly resolves in Unpaywall to a different paper (Wang & Parent, NRP frameworks). The DOI must be re-verified at the BMJ side before this citation enters any JPEP bibliography.

**Verbatim load-bearing quote (body text):**
> "Generative AI can now separate these functions, creating the possibility that, in some cases, neither the human contributor nor the AI would individually satisfy traditional authorship criteria."

**Position as evidenced by the body.** As JME Co-Editor-in-Chief, Earp et al. argue that the existing authorship concept — historically a unified cluster of ideation, prose composition, approval, and accountability — is being *disaggregated* by generative AI in much the way medicine had to disaggregate "death" into brain death and circulatory death when mechanical ventilation pulled the previously unified state apart. They present five candidate models of authorship (composition, ideation, direction, engagement, accountability), argue that none is individually adequate, and launch a formal call for commentaries on the question. The piece is **diagnostic, not prescriptive**: it claims the conceptual framework on which journals are currently building AI policy "may no longer be viable," and treats the resolution as institutionally urgent but presently unsettled.

The authors include a Generative AI Use Declaration in the editorial itself (Claude Opus 4.6 used for editing and elaboration), which the body explicitly flags as performative — the piece demonstrates the very problem it is theorising.

### 2.2 Porsdam Mann, Earp, Møller, Vynn & Savulescu (2023) — "AUTOGEN"

**Citation.** Porsdam Mann, S., Earp, B. D., Møller, N., Vynn, S., & Savulescu, J. (2023). AUTOGEN: A personalized large language model for academic enhancement — ethics and proof of principle. *The American Journal of Bioethics*, 23(10), 28–41. [10.1080/15265161.2023.2233356](https://doi.org/10.1080/15265161.2023.2233356).
**Accessibility.** Full text read via Academia.edu mirror (CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0, pre-publication version): [academia.edu/104478807](https://www.academia.edu/104478807).

**Verbatim load-bearing quote (body text):**
> "If associated ethical issues are appropriately addressed, AUTOGEN alone or in combination with other LLMs can be seen as a potent form of academic enhancement."

**Position as evidenced by the body.** The paper proposes and defends AUTOGEN — a GPT-3 model fine-tuned on a researcher's own prior work — as a form of legitimate academic enhancement. The authors (themselves AUTOGEN training subjects) draw the central distinction between a **Servant Model** (passive acceptance of AI output) and a **Co-Creation Model** (active human direction, vetting, and elaboration), and defend the latter. The ethical demand structure has three pillars: (i) transparency is necessary but not sufficient; (ii) substantial human contribution is required; (iii) "costly commitment" to evaluation and elaboration is required to convert AI output into legitimate scholarly product. The authorship question is **explicitly left open**: a model trained on the researcher's own writing sits between tool and co-author; the authors decline to resolve the metaphysics but operationally treat it as tool/enhancement.

*Nature Machine Intelligence* 7, 1889–1890 (2025). [10.1038/s42256-025-01159-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-01159-8). **Paywalled, no OA mirror found.** Reported thesis (from abstract layer only): even full good-faith disclosure cannot resolve the breakdown in scholarly acknowledgment LLMs create, because their training distributes the influence of any specific text across billions of opaque parameters. **This is the piece JPEP would most want to cite directly**; until full text is accessed, JPEP can describe Earp's position in summary form but cannot quote it.

## 4. JPEP alignment and non-overlapping concerns

### 4.1 Alignment

- **§2.1 directly supports JPEP's "essentially contested" framing.** Earp et al.'s disaggregation argument and the call for commentaries are independent evidence that the authorship concept's adequacy in the AI age is institutionally unsettled — exactly the Gallian situation JPEP exploits.
- **§2.2's "transparency necessary but not sufficient" maps onto JPEP's inadequacy-of-yes/no-disclosure argument**, though the AUTOGEN paper grounds the insufficiency in *quality-of-human-contribution* rather than in the contested-value premise. The two arguments are compatible; JPEP can cite AUTOGEN as ratifying the necessity-but-insufficiency structure without inheriting its specific welfare-economic and proof-of-principle framing.
- **§3.5 is real-world institutional movement in JPEP's direction** — JME, under Earp's co-editorship, is implementing structured mandatory disclosure that goes beyond the ICMJE yes/no flag. Whether or not the specific editorial title is "Normalising transparency," the *policy* exists and is publicly reported.
- **§3.1 (the provenance-problem paper)** independently diagnoses a structural inadequacy in disclosure-based frameworks — from an *epistemic credit / attribution* angle distinct from JPEP's *contested-value* angle. JPEP can cite Earp as an ally diagnosing the same shape of problem from a different premise, once the body text is verified.

### 4.2 Non-overlapping concerns

The verified Earp corpus does not, on reflection, contain claims that directly oppose JPEP's argument. The earlier draft of this section identified three "tensions"; on re-reading, each turns out to be a difference of axis rather than of position:

- *Earp does not treat ethics/moral-philosophy writing as categorically different from other academic writing.* True, but JPEP does not need him to; JPEP can argue the categorical-difference point on its own.
- *Earp's grounding is methodological and credit-based, not agent-integrity-based.* True, but this is parallel work, not opposing work; JPEP's Williams-grounded argument is space not contested by Earp.
- *Earp's "Authorship Without Writing" piece argues LLM-directed authorship already satisfies ICMJE.* This was misread in the earlier draft as a challenge JPEP must answer. It is not. JPEP does not advance a theory of authorship at all (see §4.3); Earp's authorship claim and JPEP's transparency claim do not occupy the same conceptual space.

### 4.3 Why JPEP and Earp do not collide

Earp's axis is authorship and credit: who counts as author, how credit and blame distribute, what disclosure attests. JPEP's axis is reader-facing process transparency: what the reader is given to judge with. The two are not competing answers to one question; they are answers to different questions.

More strongly: JPEP's transparency principle subsumes the authorship question rather than competing with positions on it. The verdicts Earp considers — that LLM-directed authorship satisfies ICMJE (senior-author analogy), that authorship is disaggregating ("Death, Authorship, Generative AI"), that substantial human contribution + vetting is constitutive (AUTOGEN) — are exactly the kind of contested positions a reader with adequate process documentation is in a position to judge between. JPEP does not need to pick one. JPEP's claim is that the picking is not the journal's job; it is the reader's, and process transparency is what makes the reader's picking epistemically possible.

Authorship is one of the contested questions a thick process disclosure enables the reader to settle. The value of the philosophical text is another. They are not the same contested question, but they are the same kind of contested question — and JPEP's transparency principle responds to the kind, not to either individually.

### 4.4 "Authorship Without Writing" as ally at the engagement layer

The Hurshman/Porsdam Mann/Savulescu/Earp argument is a principled rebuttal to the implicit norm that the human must literally write the words to be the author — the norm that appears to have driven the *Philosophy & Technology* desk-rejection. Earp et al. show that even within current ICMJE authorship criteria, the senior-author analogy already permits LLM-directed authorship. **For JPEP this is a tool at the editorial-engagement layer, not a citation for the paper body.** JPEP should not import a theory of authorship into its argument; doing so would convert one of the contested questions JPEP devolves to the reader into a question JPEP itself answers, weakening the transparency claim. The senior-author analogy is appropriate material for cover letters, reviewer-objection responses, and post-rejection routing notes — not for the paper text.

## 5. A narrow functional counter to the provenance-problem argument (JPEP-constructed, drawing on Cordasco)

⚠ **Honest provenance.** Cordasco has not, to the best of an exhaustive search, written directly on attribution in AI-assisted writing. The argument below is **JPEP's extrapolation from a Cordasco passage on peer review** to the case of authored scholarship. Cordasco should be cited for the source passage; the extension is ours.

**Source.** Cordasco, C. L. (2026, March 19). "We Need to Talk About Peer-Review and AI." *Paperclips and Other Alignment Problems* (Substack). [carlolc.substack.com/p/we-need-to-talk-about-peer-review](https://carlolc.substack.com/p/we-need-to-talk-about-peer-review).

**Verbatim quote.**
> "the question of whether the reviewer's opinions are 'genuinely hers' in some deep metaphysical sense is, at best, orthogonal to whether the review does its job."

**The narrow functional counter.** Earp's provenance-problem argument rests on a premise about what scholarly attribution is *for*: that attribution exists to track the chain of intellectual influence from prior work to present claim, and that LLM use breaks this chain in a way no disclosure norm can repair. Cordasco's peer-review passage, applied analogically, denies that premise for the function it addresses. If what matters about a referee report is whether it does its functional job — identifies the paper's strengths, flags its weaknesses, helps the editor decide — then provenance-tracking becomes a secondary concern, not a load-bearing one. **JPEP's extension:** the same logic, transposed to authored scholarship, suggests that *if* the function of scholarly authorship is the production of texts that contribute to ongoing inquiry, *then* untraceable training-data influence is only a problem to the extent it impairs that function, not in itself. The provenance problem would thereby reduce to either a quality problem (does the resulting text contribute?) or a fraud problem (does the text mislead about its production?) — both of which existing norms can in principle address.

**Why this counter does not refute Earp, and why it sharpens JPEP.** The functional reduction is contested: it assumes that what scholarship is for can be settled by appeal to function, and Earp's provenance argument is precisely an attempt to show that this assumption misses something about the chain of credit and intellectual lineage. But the *contest* between Earp's chain-of-credit framing and Cordasco's function-orthogonal-to-provenance framing is itself an instance of JPEP's central claim: there is no settled professional consensus about what attribution is for in the AI age. JPEP can use this dialectic as evidence that yes/no disclosure cannot rest on a stable view of what AI use does to attribution, because the field has not converged on what attribution is doing.
