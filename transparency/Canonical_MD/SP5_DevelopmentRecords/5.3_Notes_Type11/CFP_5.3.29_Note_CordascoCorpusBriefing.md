## The Core Argument

Across the 12 posts, Cordasco is constructing what can be called **welfare-accounting humility** in AI governance. The argument has three components:

**(a) Theoretical claim** (most rigorously developed in Posts 10 and 11). Formal welfare analyses that take current competencies as baseline cannot see gains from emergent reorganization of those competencies, because those gains are constitutively outside the existing representational apparatus. The asymmetry is not epistemological (we could measure them if we tried harder) but ontological (they don't yet exist when the analysis is performed). Cordasco draws on Karni and Vierø's work on unawareness and offers a critique of Acemoglu et al.'s "garbling" model.

**(b) Autobiographical demonstration** (Posts 7 and 1). The transformations AI produced in his own research practice — specifically in question-identification and portfolio management — were prospectively invisible and only retrospectively legible. Cordasco cannot make this argument from others' data because the data does not exist; he can only model what such evidence would look like from inside.

**(c) Institutional implications** (Posts 6, 8, 9, 5). Governance frameworks should default toward permissive experimentation, preserve conditions for emergent benefits, and invest in designing institutional architectures (principal hierarchies, social utility functions, peer oversight structures) that allow value to crystallize through experience rather than being specified in advance.

## Why Posts 10 and 11 Are the Cited Pair

The two posts cited in §3 v4 articulate the sharpest version of the theoretical claim — the version that engages JPEP's transparency framework most directly:

- **Post 11, "The Invisible Upside of Cognitive Offloading" (1 February 2026) → Cordasco 2026a.** The asymmetry between observable costs (skill decay) and unobservable benefits (practices that don't yet exist). Plato correctly identified writing's memory costs but couldn't foresee the scholarly infrastructure it enabled. The Kreps-style flexibility-preference argument under Knightian uncertainty: when the space of possibilities is being transformed by the technology, rational agents prefer larger option sets, and institutions should preserve flexibility rather than locking in particular paths.

- **Post 10, "Acemoglu et al (2026) are wrong about AI & Human Cognition" (2 March 2026) → Cordasco 2026b.** The architectural critique of Acemoglu et al.'s formal model proposing "garbling" (deliberately limiting AI precision to preserve human learning). The model is mathematically sound but architecturally self-defeating: it treats as fixed exogenous parameters the very representational apparatus through which knowledge and competence are defined, thereby excluding by construction the mechanisms most likely to complicate its policy conclusion. The cockpit-automation analogy: the model could measure pilots losing stick-and-rudder skills but "cannot see Crew Resource Management" — the new discipline of team cognition that emerged from changed automation conditions. The printing-press analogy: the model is like "analysing the printing press under the assumption that the only books worth reading are the ones that existed in 1440."

Together, these two posts articulate the welfare-economic-architectural objection that §3 v4's "A welfare-economic challenge" paragraph paraphrases.

## Posts Considered and Not Cited (With Reasons)

- **Post 3 ("On Lost...", 19 April 2026)** — distinguishes the Locke and Jack positions on philosophical engagement with AI. JPEP is on the Locke side. **Considered as a potential ally citation** for the Introduction's gap claim. **Rejected** under the user's directive to treat Cordasco only as objector.
- **Post 4 ("AI can learn taste", 23 March 2026)** — argues that AI systems can be trained to predict high-impact research via citation data, but that this achievement is self-undermining because scientific taste is not a fixed target. Parallel structure to JPEP's essentially-contested argument applied to citation-prediction models. **Considered as a sympathetic-interlocutor citation.** **Rejected** under the same directive.
- **Post 5 ("We Need to Talk About Peer-Review and AI", 19 March 2026)** — defends using LLMs while preparing peer reviews on the grounds that the Condorcet independence condition is inter-reviewer, not intra-reviewer. The closest direct engagement with academic AI norms in the corpus. Argues about *permissibility* of AI use, not about *transparency*. Compatible with JPEP rather than in tension with it (JPEP could be read as: "use AI freely, but be transparent about it"). **Not cited** because the §3 engagement focused on the welfare-architectural critique, where Posts 10 and 11 are the sharpest source.
- **Posts 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12** — not directly relevant to §3's argument about AI transparency in ethics research. Documented in the table for completeness; their summaries are in the subagent task output (see Provenance below) and could be retrieved if a future revision needs to engage them.
